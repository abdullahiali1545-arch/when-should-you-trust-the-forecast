London air pollution forecasts, logged before the readings are published

A fixed model forecasts fine-particle pollution (PM2.5) six hours ahead at four London monitoring sites. Each forecast is saved to a public log before the official reading comes out, then checked against that reading once it is published.

PM2.5 is measured in micrograms per cubic metre of air (µg/m³). For scale, the World Health Organization's guideline for a 24-hour average is 15 µg/m³.

How the forecasts have done so far

Error is the average gap between a forecast and the reading it was checked against, in µg/m³. Lower is better. The no-change forecast simply assumes the level six hours later will be the same as now. It is the bar any real model has to beat.

The six forecasts from one night are closely linked, so a night counts as roughly one piece of evidence, not six. I committed in advance not to draw conclusions from this table until at least 26 weeks have been scored, including a full January to March.

How the forecasts are made

The model is LightGBM, a gradient-boosted decision tree method. It was trained once on 2018 to 2025 data from each site, using the previous day of pollution and weather readings, and has not been retrained since. The live log is therefore a test of the same model that was evaluated in the project.

Rebuilt from its saved settings, the model reproduces the evaluated forecasts exactly, on both Windows and Linux.

What the amber warning means

A site turns amber when the model's average error over the previous 24 hours is above a limit fixed for that site before the first live forecast. It is a reason to treat the current number with more caution. It does not change the forecast.

In the project's backtest, a more complex warning model was not reliably better than this simple rule. It did no better at Marylebone Road or on the 2025 holdout year, and did better at the other three sites only in the main test. Switching to the no-change forecast whenever a warning fired made overall error worse, which is why this page warns rather than switches.

Why only night-time hours are scored

DEFRA publishes readings once a day, usually 8 to 15 hours after they are taken. When the logger runs in the morning, the newest reading is from around 11pm. A forecast only counts if its own reading has not yet been published, so in practice only forecasts for roughly midnight to 6am can be logged in time.

The live scores therefore cover night-time hours, when pollution is usually lower and steadier. They are not directly comparable with the backtest, which scored every hour of the day.

Nothing is edited afterwards

Forecasts and scores are written to separate files that can only be added to, on a branch that blocks rewrites. Every rule used on this page was committed to the project specification before the first live forecast was made.

Code and full write-up are in the project repository. The raw live log and the pre-registered specification are public.
